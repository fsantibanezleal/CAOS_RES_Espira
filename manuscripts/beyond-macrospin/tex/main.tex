\begin{abstract}
Optimal control of magnetization switching has been solved almost entirely for a single rotating
moment, and its authors state in print that it remains open under what conditions nonuniform
mechanisms such as domain-wall nucleation and propagation become optimal in terms of energy
efficiency. We answer this for a ferromagnetic spin chain with nearest-neighbour exchange by minimizing
the switching cost over the trajectory of every site, with no assumed reversal mode. Two results
follow. First, a lower bound: inverting the equation of motion and applying the arithmetic-geometric
mean inequality gives $\Phi \ge 4\alpha\,\Delta E/(\gamma\mu)$ for every pulse at every switching time,
with $\Delta E$ the minimum-energy-path barrier; it equals the known infinite-time optimum for a single
uniaxial moment. Second, above a crossover length and at long switching time the free optimum is a
domain wall entering at an open end, strictly cheaper than uniform rotation. Over 61 chains ($J/K=10$,
$N$ from 4 to 32, two damping values) 28 reverse more cheaply through a wall, by up to 51 percent, and
every cost lies above its barrier floor. The saving at fixed switching time is not monotone in
length: a longer wall needs more time to cross. One case was verified by grid refinement, local
consistency, and open-loop forward dynamics. This version corrects version 1, whose comparison of two
fixed modes concluded that uniform rotation is always the field-cost optimum.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Correction to version 1}

Version 1 compared a uniform rotation with a constant-speed domain wall at a short switching time and
concluded that uniform rotation is the field-cost optimum, adding that walls lower the energy barrier
but not the field cost. Both statements are withdrawn. The comparison was correct for the two modes and
the short time it tested, but the constant-speed wall is not the optimal wall, and the free search
reported here finds wall-mediated reversals cheaper than uniform rotation at long switching times. The
barrier floor of Section~\ref{sec:floor} shows that the energy barrier and the field cost are linked,
not opposed.

\section{Introduction}

Optimal control theory computes the applied-field pulse that reverses a magnetic moment at the least
cost~\citep{kwiatkowski2021}, and applied to two-dimensional van der Waals magnets it reaches
picosecond, energy-efficient switching~\citep{badarneh2026}. The formalism has been developed almost
entirely for a single moment on the unit sphere. Its authors note the limitation: ``A macrospin
approximation is used in the present study, but this is expected to break down with increasing system
size. Even if the initial and the final states are collinear, the transition between them may involve
nonuniform rotation of magnetization such as nucleation and propagation of domain walls or excitation
of spin waves. It remains to be seen under what conditions these and possibly other, yet unknown
switching mechanisms become optimal in terms of energy efficiency''~\citep{badarneh2023}. The only step
past the macrospin we know of is a one-dimensional study that found a standing spin wave in the optimal
reversal above a critical length~\citep{badarneh2020}.

We minimize the switching cost of a spin chain over all trajectories, derive a barrier floor under the
cost, and map where the optimum stops being uniform.

\section{Model and cost}

A chain of $N$ unit moments $\vec s_i$, each of moment $\mu$, has energy
\begin{equation}
E = -K \sum_i s_{i,z}^2 \;-\; J \sum_{\langle ij\rangle} \vec s_i \cdot \vec s_j ,
\end{equation}
with easy-axis anisotropy $K$ per site, ferromagnetic exchange $J$, and open ends. The internal field
at a site is $\vec b^{\,\mathrm{int}}_i = -\mu^{-1}\partial E/\partial \vec s_i$. Given any trajectory,
the Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert equation is inverted at every site for the applied field that site needs,
\begin{equation}
\vec b_i = \frac{\alpha}{\gamma}\dot{\vec s}_i + \frac{1}{\gamma}\,\vec s_i\times\dot{\vec s}_i
- \vec b^{\,\mathrm{int}}_{i,\perp},
\label{eq:inverse}
\end{equation}
and the switching cost is $\Phi = \int_0^T \sum_i |\vec b_i|^2\,dt$, in T$^2$~s. It is not an energy;
converting it to joules needs a circuit model, which we do not assume. Times are in units of
$\tau_0 = \mu/(2\gamma K)$.

\section{A barrier floor under the switching cost}
\label{sec:floor}

Write $P_i = -\mu\,\vec b^{\,\mathrm{int}}_i\cdot\dot{\vec s}_i$ for the power the internal field
absorbs at site $i$, so that $\sum_i P_i = dE/dt$. Dotting Eq.~\eqref{eq:inverse} with
$\dot{\vec s}_i$, and using $\dot{\vec s}_i \perp \vec s_i$, gives
\begin{equation}
\mu\,\vec b_i\cdot\dot{\vec s}_i = P_i + \mu\frac{\alpha}{\gamma}|\dot{\vec s}_i|^2 .
\end{equation}
Only the component of $\vec b_i$ along $\dot{\vec s}_i$ enters, so $|\vec b_i|^2 \ge (\vec b_i\cdot
\dot{\vec s}_i/|\dot{\vec s}_i|)^2$. Where $P_i>0$, the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality applied to
the two terms gives $|\vec b_i|^2 \ge 4\alpha P_i/(\gamma\mu)$, and trivially $|\vec b_i|^2 \ge 0$
elsewhere. Summing over sites, $\sum_i |\vec b_i|^2 \ge (4\alpha/\gamma\mu)\max(dE/dt,0)$, and
integrating, the cost is at least $4\alpha/(\gamma\mu)$ times the total energy climbed. Every path from
all up to all down passes through a configuration at least as high as the saddle of the minimum energy
path, so it climbs at least that barrier $\Delta E$, and
\begin{equation}
\Phi \;\ge\; \frac{4\alpha}{\gamma\mu}\,\Delta E
\label{eq:floor}
\end{equation}
for every pulse and every switching time. For one uniaxial moment $\Delta E = K$ and the bound equals
the infinite-time optimum $\Phi_\infty = 4\alpha K/(\gamma\mu)$ of Ref.~\citep{kwiatkowski2021}, so it is
tight there. Equality needs the field parallel to the velocity and the precession supplied by the
internal field; uniform rotation meets both at long times, and whether a wall can is not guaranteed.

We compute $\Delta E$ with the climbing-image string method~\citep{e2007} in the geodesic form used for
magnetic systems~\citep{bessarab2015}. For $J/K = 10$ short chains reverse coherently
($\Delta E = NK$); above about eight sites a wall entering at an open end has the lower barrier, which
saturates at $8.867\,K$ against the continuum wall energy $2\sqrt{2JK} = 8.944\,K$. The last row of
Table~\ref{tab:alpha05} gives $\Delta E/(NK)$.

\section{Free optimal control of the chain}

The image-based direct minimization of Ref.~\citep{badarneh2023} is generalized to $N$ coupled sites:
the trajectory is $Q+2$ time slices of $N$ unit vectors with clamped ends, the midpoint rule gives
position and velocity on every interval and site, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:inverse} with the chain internal
field gives the cost. The exact central-difference gradient is obtained in 36 cost evaluations
independent of $Q$ and $N$ by graph coloring: an image touches two intervals in time and three sites in
space, so images two apart in time and three apart in space are perturbed together. The cost is
minimized by L-BFGS on normalized vectors from three starts, the single-site optimum on every site with a
small perturbation, a tanh wall on the optimum's azimuth, and the minimum energy path traversed on the
optimum's schedule. Uniform rotation is feasible and never pays exchange, so the uniform cost on the
same grid is an exact upper bound on the optimum, and every cost we report is the cost of an explicit
feasible trajectory: an upper bound on the true optimum even where the optimizer stopped early.

\section{Results}

Tables~\ref{tab:alpha05} and~\ref{tab:alpha01} give the cheapest trajectory found relative to uniform
rotation. Three regimes appear.

\emph{Short chains.} For $N \le 6$ at $J/K = 10$ the barrier is $NK$ and uniform rotation is optimal at
every switching time: walls cannot lower the barrier, and no start finds a cheaper trajectory.

\emph{Short switching times.} At $T \le 20\,\tau_0$ uniform rotation is optimal at every length even
where the floor leaves large room. The wall has no time to cross the chain: its propagation cost grows
with its speed.

\emph{Long chains at long times.} Above the barrier crossover and at long switching times the optimum is
nonuniform and strictly cheaper. At $\alpha = 0.5$ the saving reaches 51 percent ($N=24$,
$T=160\,\tau_0$), against a floor of 0.324 of the uniform cost. At $\alpha = 0.1$ the regime starts
later, near $150\,\tau_0$, because the uniform optimum approaches its own infinite-time floor on a time
scale that grows as the damping falls; at $300\,\tau_0$ a sixteen-site chain saves 29 percent.

At fixed switching time the saving is not monotone in length. At $\alpha = 0.5$, $T = 40\,\tau_0$ it is
largest at $N = 16$ and shrinks for longer chains, and the optimal length moves up as $T$ grows. A
longer chain has a lower barrier per site but a longer distance for the wall to cross in the same time.
Over all 61 chains every cost lies above its floor, the closest at 1.018 times it.

\section{Verification of a sub-uniform trajectory}

Because the result reverses a published conclusion, one case ($N=16$, $J/K=10$, $\alpha=0.1$,
$T=150\,\tau_0$) was checked three independent ways before being accepted. Its entry in
Table~\ref{tab:alpha01} (0.934) comes from the bake's automatically chosen grid and fixed iteration
budget; longer solves on finer grids lower it, which is why the values below differ. The
minimum-energy-path start on a 300-slice grid gave 0.892 of the uniform cost.
\emph{Grid refinement:} interpolated to 600 slices and re-optimized, the ratio fell to 0.838 against the
600-slice uniform cost; a discretization artifact would have shrunk.
\emph{Local consistency:} integrating every interval from its left image under its own midpoint field
with a fine Runge-Kutta step and comparing with its right image gives errors of the same order for the
wall trajectory (maximum $4.1\times10^{-3}$, mean $7.1\times10^{-4}$) as for the uniform optimum
(maximum $2.0\times10^{-3}$, mean $6.5\times10^{-4}$) on the same grid.
\emph{Open-loop dynamics:} applying the recovered per-site fields of the 600-slice trajectory to the
chain equation of motion from the all-up state reverses every site (final $s_z = -1.000$ on all
sixteen) and tracks the optimized trajectory to within 0.106. A second, independent start, the tanh
wall alone, also lands below uniform rotation for this chain.

Three numerical traps were found on the way and are guarded in the code: the geodesic angle between
nearly coincident images must be computed as $2\arctan(|\vec r-\vec l|/|\vec r+\vec l|)$ rather than
$\arccos(\vec l\cdot\vec r)$, whose rounding error made the finite-difference gradient noise; the
optimizer's relative stopping test must not be scaled by an expensive seed's own cost; and a wall seed
must not compress the precession into each site's flip window, which creates near-antipodal jumps where
the midpoint rule is singular.

\section{Discussion and limits}

The floor explains why the answer depends on length: the cost of any reversal is at least proportional
to the barrier it must climb, and a wall entering at an open end climbs a barrier that stops growing
with length while uniform rotation climbs $NK$. Whether a pulse can approach the floor is a question
of time, because the wall must also be moved across the chain. The optimal reversal is therefore
uniform for short chains or short times and wall-mediated for long chains at long times, with an
optimal length that grows with the switching time.

The trajectories reported are the cheapest found, not certified global optima; most nonuniform solves
stopped before their convergence criterion, so the true savings are at least those quoted. The study is
one-dimensional, with nearest-neighbour exchange, a uniaxial anisotropy, and open ends; the floor holds
in any dimension, but the crossover map is specific to this model. The dimensionless map depends only on
$J/K$, $\alpha$, $T/\tau_0$ and $N$; material parameters enter through $\tau_0$ and the cost scale.
